# Predicting Respiratory Hospital Admissions from Air Quality

A data mining course project. We asked whether air quality can predict how many people are admitted to hospital with respiratory problems each day, and whether more complex machine-learning models do better than simple ones, across eight cities.

> **Important: the dataset is synthetic.** Our own checks showed that the data was generated by a computer program, not recorded in real hospitals. For example, its "dates" run for 242 years, one row per day, and all eight cities have identical pollution levels. So this project is about **how prediction models behave on a known benchmark**, not about real health effects. Nothing here should be quoted as evidence about any real city.

## The question in plain words
Hospitals would like warning of busy days. Air pollution, especially fine particles (PM2.5), is known to affect breathing. We tested:
1. How well daily admissions can be predicted from air-quality and weather measurements.
2. Whether advanced models (support vector machines, neural networks) beat a simple straight-line model.
3. Whether cities differ.
4. Which measurements matter.
5. Whether unusually busy "spike" days can be flagged in advance.

## What we did
- **Checked the data first.** Before building any model, we audited the data. That revealed it is synthetic, that only PM2.5 is related to admissions, and that the cities are statistically identical.
- **Held back a test set.** 20% of the days were locked away and used **only once**, at the very end, to score the final models fairly.
- **Built four kinds of model** (Linear Regression, a Decision Tree, a Support Vector Machine and a Neural Network) and tuned each one carefully on the other 80%, following established guidance from the research literature.
- **Compared every model against simple baselines,** such as "always predict the average" or "use PM2.5 alone".
- **Measured uncertainty** for every result, and decided in advance how big a difference must be before calling one model better.

## What we found
- **All models perform the same.** They all explain about 15% of the day-to-day variation in admissions, which is the maximum this data allows. The neural network and the support vector machine are no better than a straight line on PM2.5.
- **PM2.5 is the only thing that matters.** Adding the other pollutants, weather and city adds nothing.
- **Cities behave the same.** A separate model for each city is no better than one shared model.
- **Busy days can be flagged, but only weakly.** The models spot spike days about twice as well as chance, but to catch half of them they must flag roughly one day in four.
- **A detail that confirms the data is artificial:** admissions rise in exact steps of about one extra patient for every 10 units of PM2.5. Real pollution effects are smooth.

**Why this is a useful result:** when the data contains one simple signal plus a lot of noise, extra model complexity buys nothing. Knowing that requires a careful check of the data, simple baselines, and a fair final test, and those are the skills this project demonstrates.
