# Results Summary: What the Model Comparison Shows

A plain-language summary of the project's results, with the key figures. For the full report see [REPORT.md](REPORT.md); for every step in detail see [../docs/METHODOLOGY.md](../docs/METHODOLOGY.md).

> **Reminder:** the dataset is synthetic (generated by a program), so these results describe how the models behave on this data, not real health effects.

---

## The short answer
**All four models are equally accurate.** Linear Regression, the Decision Tree, the Support Vector Machine (SVM) and the Neural Network (MLP) all predict daily admissions about as well as each other, and about as well as a straight line drawn against PM2.5 alone.

**So the comparison isn't decided by accuracy. It's decided by everything else:** how long each model takes to build, whether you can explain it, what it actually learned, and how reliable it is. On those points the models differ clearly, and **the simple models win.** They match the complex ones while being faster, easier to explain and just as reliable. In this kind of data, extra complexity costs a lot and buys nothing.

---

## 1. Accuracy: a tie
Every model was tested once, at the end, on 17,698 days it had never seen. The figure shows each model's test error (RMSE, lower is better) with its 95% confidence interval.

![Test error of every model with 95% confidence intervals](../results/figures/09_test_rmse_ci.png)

*Figure: test error (RMSE) with 95% confidence intervals. The dashed line is the simplest model, a straight line on PM2.5 alone. The grey band is the tie margin we fixed before seeing any results: models inside it count as equally good.*

**What it shows:**
- **Every model sits inside the tie band.** None is meaningfully better than the simple straight line.
- **The intervals overlap almost completely.** The differences between models are far smaller than the uncertainty in measuring them.
- **The Decision Tree is a hair better.** The gap is consistent enough to be real, but far too small to matter in practice. Section 3 explains where it comes from.
- **The neural network is a hair worse.** It picks up a little noise from the inputs that don't matter.

**Why they all tie:** most of the day-to-day change in admissions in this data is random. No input can predict it, so every model hits the same ceiling. They explain about 15% of the variation, and that's all there is to explain.

## 2. The comparison: what differs when accuracy ties

| What we compare | Linear Regression | Decision Tree | SVM | Neural network (MLP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** (test RMSE; lower is better) | 3.420: same as the straight-line baseline | 3.409: a hair better (Section 3) | 3.420: same | 3.425: a hair worse |
| **Time to train one model** | under 1 second | about 1 second | 4–15 seconds, even on a 20,000-row subset | 4–28 seconds (5 copies needed) |
| **Time spent tuning in this project** | seconds | about 50 min (our choice of a full grid; a smaller search would take minutes) | about 1 h 15 min | about 1 h 45 min |
| **Can you explain how it decides?** | **Yes.** One number per input, e.g. "+1 admission per 10 units of PM2.5" | **Yes.** A readable set of if-then rules ([tree diagram](../results/figures/12_tree_structure.png)) | No. Thousands of stored data points | No. Thousands of internal weights |
| **What shape it learned** | A straight line | **The true staircase** (Section 3) | A straight line | A straight line |
| **Which inputs it uses** | PM2.5 (all others ≈ zero) | **Only** PM2.5 | PM2.5 | PM2.5, plus tiny amounts of noise from the others |
| **Spike-day ranking** (PR-AUC; higher is better) | 0.249 | 0.238: slightly worse | 0.249: same as Linear | 0.247: same |
| **Spike-day alarm** (F1; higher is better) | 0.314 | 0.311 | 0.295: its alarm cut-off transferred badly to new data | 0.313 |
| **Same answer every time?** | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes (5 random starts gave nearly identical results) |
| **Needs a lot of data?** | No | No | No (as good with 5,000 rows as with 40,000) | No |

**Reading the table:**
- **Accuracy is a four-way tie,** so it can't pick a winner.
- **Linear Regression is the best practical choice.** It's as accurate as anything else, trains instantly, and anyone can read it.
- **The Decision Tree is the most informative model.** It's the only one that discovered the data's true shape, and its rules are readable.
- **The SVM and neural network cost by far the most computing time and are hard to explain,** yet they found nothing the simple models missed.

## 3. What each model actually learned
This figure is the clearest single picture of the comparison. It shows how each model's prediction changes as PM2.5 rises, with everything else held fixed.

![How each model's prediction changes with PM2.5](../results/figures/12_partial_dependence.png)

*Figure: predicted admissions as PM2.5 increases, for each model. The short black lines show the actual average admissions in each 10-unit band of PM2.5.*

**What it shows:**
- **Linear Regression, the SVM and the neural network all draw the same straight line.** The two "smart" models, given full freedom, rediscovered the simple line and nothing more.
- **The Decision Tree draws a staircase,** and the staircase matches the real data (the black lines). In this data, admissions jump by about one patient each time PM2.5 crosses 10, 20, 30, …; they don't rise smoothly. The tree fits steps like this naturally, and that's the source of its tiny accuracy edge.
- **The staircase is also proof the data is artificial.** Real pollution effects are smooth; exact steps at round numbers are a sign of a computer-generated dataset.

## 4. Which inputs matter
We shuffled each input in turn and measured how much worse the predictions became. If shuffling an input does nothing, the model wasn't using it.

![Permutation importance](../results/figures/12_permutation_importance.png)

*Figure: how much the error grows when each input is shuffled (bigger bar = more important).*

**What it shows:**
- **Only PM2.5 matters.** Shuffling it makes predictions clearly worse.
- **Shuffling anything else** (other pollutants, AQI, temperature, humidity, city) changes almost nothing.
- **This confirms our main hypothesis (H1):** PM2.5 alone predicts as well as all the inputs together. Models given only PM2.5 were exactly as accurate as models given everything.

## 5. Do the cities differ?
We compared one shared model against a separate model for each city, each tuned on that city's own data.

![PM2.5 effect by city](../results/figures/10_city_slopes.png)

*Figure: the estimated effect of PM2.5 in each city, with 95% confidence intervals. The dashed line is the effect for all cities together.*

**What it shows:**
- **Separate city models were never better** than one shared model.
- **The effect of PM2.5 is nearly the same everywhere.** Small differences are detectable with this much data, but they're too small to matter. The two smallest cities (São Paulo and Cairo) have the widest intervals.

---

## 6. Can busy days be flagged in advance?
A "spike" day is one of the busiest 10% of days.
- **Better than guessing:** every model flags spike days about twice as well as random guessing.
- **But weak in practice:** to catch about half of the spike days, a model has to raise the alarm on about one day in four, and only about one alarm in four or five is a real spike.
- **Risk rises with PM2.5:** below about 20 units, spikes are rare (around 3 in 100 days); above about 60 units, they happen on roughly a third of days.

## 7. So which model should you choose?

| If you need… | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| A practical predictor | **Linear Regression** | As accurate as any model, instant, fully explainable |
| To understand the data's shape | **Decision Tree** | The only model that found the staircase; readable rules |
| Maximum accuracy | Any of them | They tie, so complexity buys nothing here |
| SVM or neural network | Not recommended here | Hours of computing, no gain, hard to explain |

---

## 8. Why this is a useful result
- **"No model beats the simple one" is a real finding** when the comparison is fair. Here every model got thorough tuning, one untouched test, and confidence intervals, so the tie can't be blamed on lazy tuning or lucky test data.
- **It matches published research.** A systematic review of medical prediction studies (Christodoulou et al., 2019) found that machine learning was no better than simple logistic regression in fair comparisons. Our project shows the same thing, and shows *why*: there's only one simple signal to learn.
- **The project caught a problem others missed.** This dataset is presented as real health data, but our checks show it's generated. Anyone building a model on it should know that.
- **The take-away for practice:** check the data first, always compare against a simple baseline, and choose the simplest model that does the job.
